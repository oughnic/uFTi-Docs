# Calendar examples from the diary of Abraham De la Pryme

Evidence for the examples in the topics on old-style dates. This is a narrow exception to the rule that examples come from the Brontë dataset (DEC-202). It is not a second dataset: it holds eleven claims, each with its evidence, and nothing else. No file of these dates is kept or shipped (DEC-219).

Part of the [decision log](../../../authoring/decision-log.md). Written 5 November 2026. Extended 9 November 2026 (DEC-219).

## 1. Why a second source

Every date in the Brontë dataset falls after 1752, so it is all Gregorian. The Julian calendar, a year that began on 25 March and a year written with two numbers need a source from before the change. Abraham De la Pryme (1671 to 1704) was a Yorkshire diarist and antiquary. His diary was printed in 1870, and the printed edition is on the Internet Archive.

## 4. What the claims show

- **P1 to P4.** If Abraham's year began on 25 March, January 1671 would be January 1672 by our count. His brother would then be born about three months later, which cannot be. So he began his year on 1 January, and his birth year is 1671 whichever way it is counted. Before 25 March, a writer might use either year: that is why the old-style year matters.
- **P5 and P6.** The same early months of the year appear as 1693 in one place and as 1692-3 in another. They name the same year in two ways.
- **P7, P8 and P11.** England used the Julian calendar in his lifetime. We work out the Gregorian day ourselves; the edition does not give it. 15 January 1671 is 25 January, and 20 November 1699 is 30 November: ten days. 18 September 1700 is 29 September, and 12 June 1704 is 23 June: eleven days. The gap grew because 1700 had a 29 February in the Julian calendar and not in the Gregorian.
- **P9 and P10.** His letters, as printed, give a two-number year for a date before 25 March. His account of his birth has one number. The printed forms may be the editor's, because the edition is a derivative.
- **P11, the day of the week.** 12 June 1704 was a Monday in the Julian calendar and a Thursday in the Gregorian. So the friend's date is a Julian one. We worked out the two days of the week; the edition gives only the Monday.
- **What the diary cannot show.** Abraham died in 1704. Nothing here shows the days left out in September 1752, so that part stays as general history in the topic on calendars.
- **A library catalogue.** A catalogue may give a different birth year, by adding a year for the old-style rule. We have not read one, so no catalogue is a claim here.

The example does not settle any scholarly debate. It shows how the same year can be written in more than one way.

## 5. Privacy check

Everyone named in the topics was born in the 17th century. Nobody could be living. The topics name Abraham, his parents and his brother only. The friend whose diary gives the day of his death is not named.
